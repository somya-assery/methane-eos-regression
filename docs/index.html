Methane Equation of State by Regression

Can a regression model learn how methane behaves from experimental data, and how far can we trust it on data it has never seen?

1. The problem

An equation of state (EOS) links pressure, density and temperature of a fluid. It is needed to design compressors, pipelines and storage for natural gas. Classical EOS forms such as van der Waals or Peng–Robinson use a few fixed parameters. Here we instead learn the EOS from measurements.

Question: given temperature T and molar density ρ of methane, how accurately can regression predict the compressibility factor Z = P / (ρRT), and does it hold up on isotherms, pressure ranges and whole datasets that were not used for training?

Target: Z − 1. Inputs known at prediction time: T and ρ, written as reduced density δ = ρ/ρc and inverse reduced temperature τ = Tc/T (Tc = 190.564 K, ρc = 162.66 kg/m³). Unit of observation: one measured state point. Scope of the claim: methane only, within the temperature and pressure range of the data. Pressure follows from P = Z ρ R T.

2. Data

Four published experimental P–ρ–T datasets for methane are combined. Each row is one measured state point (T, P, ρ). All values are converted to SI units (K, Pa, mol/m³) and Z, δ and τ are computed.

3. Method

We build a bank of 36 terms δkτt (k = 1…6, t = 0…5) and fit Z − 1 = Σ ci · δkτt. Features are standardised using training data only.

ModelRole
Ideal gasBaseline, Z = 1
Virial OLSBaseline, 5 terms (δ, δτ, δτ², δ², δ²τ)
RidgeAll 36 terms, L2 penalty. Smooth and accurate
LassoL1 penalty, keeps only some terms. Compact and readable
Bayesian ridge, ARDGive predictive uncertainty

The final compact model is a lasso with α = 2×10⁻⁵ that keeps 15 of 36 terms:

Each chip is one term of the model, a power of reduced density δ (density ÷ critical density) times a power of τ (critical temperature ÷ temperature). For example δ² is density squared, and a term with no τ has τ⁰ = 1. Coefficients act on standardised features and are stored in model.json.

5. How we tested it

A model that only fits its training data proves little, so every error reported below is on unseen data.

  • Leave-one-source-out: train on three datasets, test on the fourth. This is the hardest test because a whole region may be missing.
  • Leave-one-isotherm/isochore-out: 50 groups, each a full isotherm (Cristancho, Douslin) or isochore (Goodwin & Prydz, Vennix) held out in turn.
  • High-pressure test: train below 40 MPa, test at 40 MPa and above (63 rows).
  • Near-critical test: train without the 67 near-critical rows, then test on them. The rule was fixed before seeing results.

Regularisation was tuned by cross-validation inside the training sources for the leave-one-source-out comparison. Caveat: the final lasso strength was chosen from that comparison, so the later splits are not completely independent of it.

Metric: median % error in Z, which resists a few extreme points. Worst single errors are also reported.

Reading the results

  • Regression beats both baselines almost everywhere. Typical errors fall from 8–80 % (ideal gas) to about 0.1–2 %.
  • Ridge is slightly more accurate, while the 15-term lasso is nearly as good and can be written down in one line, so we use it as the final model.
  • Predicting an entire unseen region is hard. With Goodwin & Prydz (cold liquid) held out, errors are large because nothing else covers those conditions.
  • Near the critical point, where Z changes steeply, errors rise to roughly 10–13 %.
  • Bayesian ridge uncertainty is over-confident: its 95 % intervals contained only 71 %, 100 %, 68 % and 23 % of held-out points (Cristancho, Douslin, Goodwin & Prydz, Vennix).

7. Limitations

  • Valid only for 90–450 K and up to about 188 MPa. No extrapolation.
  • Whole phase regions missing from training cannot be predicted reliably; worst single errors reach hundreds of percent for cold liquid, where Z is close to zero and % error is inflated.
  • Accuracy drops near the critical point.
  • Held-out isotherm errors (about 0.5–1.4 %) describe interpolation between neighbouring conditions, not new regions.
  • The lasso strength was picked using all four sources, so the final numbers are slightly optimistic.
  • No thermodynamic-consistency constraints are imposed, and a cubic EOS such as Peng–Robinson was not used as a baseline.

8. Conclusions

  • A sparse regression on δ and τ can reproduce methane's Z within about 1–2 % on held-out isotherms, pressures and sources that overlap the training conditions.
  • Simple baselines (ideal gas, 5-term virial) are far worse, so the extra terms are justified.
  • Generalising to unseen regions and to the critical region remains the main weakness; more data there, or physical constraints, would be the next step.
